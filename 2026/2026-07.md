# Fusion Digest — July 2026

_Run: 2026-08-09_

## 1. JET's final deuterium-tritium campaign sets a new fusion energy record, closing 40 years of operation

JET, the Joint European Torus at Culham, has completed its third and final deuterium-tritium campaign (DTE3), closing four decades of operation before entering decommissioning. Together with the USA's now-shutdown TFTR, JET has been one of only two tokamaks ever able to run on the same D-T fuel mix that will power ITER and first-of-a-kind power plants — a irreplaceable capability now retired until ITER itself reaches its D-T phase. The headline result is a new fusion energy record of 69 MJ, produced in a tritium-rich hybrid plasma scenario and the highest energy output ever from a fusion device, surpassing JET's own previous D-T record of 59 MJ set in its 2021–2022 campaign (DTE2). What changed wasn't just raw power: the team paired the energy record with an ITER-relevant integrated scenario, sustaining high confinement (H98 ≈ 0.85) at 3 MA in D-T plasmas with a partially detached divertor — demonstrating that reactor-grade confinement and controlled heat exhaust can coexist rather than trading off against each other. Two advanced exhaust regimes designed to avoid damaging transient heat loads, quasi-continuous exhaust and the X-point radiator, were established first in deuterium and then, for the first time, successfully extended to full D-T plasmas. The campaign also pushed H-mode duration to 60 seconds in D-T, extensively exercised JET's shattered pellet injector to generate data now feeding directly into the design of ITER's disruption mitigation system, and ran combined gas-balance, post-mortem and laser-induced desorption spectroscopy studies of fuel retention to inform how ITER will track its tritium inventory. Nearly 400 scientists and engineers across Europe contributed, coordinated through EUROfusion's Tokamak Exploitation Work Package, in a "stepladder" approach that extrapolated physics from medium-size tokamaks (ASDEX Upgrade, MAST-U, TCV, WEST) up to JET's scale. For the technology, the significance lies less in the single energy number than in the completeness of the dataset: with JET now the last operating D-T-capable tokamak until ITER, its final campaigns are an irreplaceable validation point for predictive models, disruption mitigation design, and tritium accounting — all directly load-bearing for ITER's licensing and operation, and for the safety case of first-of-a-kind power plants.

**Framing.** The only coverage retrieved this cycle is EUROfusion's own release, so there is no US or Asian outlet to contrast it against directly. What is notable is that even in this single account, the framing is characteristically European: the 69 MJ record is presented as secondary to the ITER-relevant integrated scenario, exhaust regimes, and tritium-retention data, with repeated emphasis on "irreplaceable" datasets and "legacy" rather than on the record as a milestone in itself. That matches the pattern Physics World and other European outlets typically apply to JET/WEST/W7-X results — methodical validation toward ITER and DEMO, not a race to a number.

## 2. ITER installs its sixth of nine tokamak sector modules, two-thirds of the core now in place, ahead of schedule

ITER has installed the sixth of nine tokamak sector modules into the tokamak pit, bringing two-thirds of the machine's torus-shaped core into place almost six months ahead of schedule. The ~1,100-tonne sector module — nearly 1,400 tonnes including its lifting rig — was transferred from the Assembly Hall and lowered into the pit in a coordinated 30-hour operation involving more than 100 people, concluding on 28 July. What changed is as much procedural as physical: each successive module lift has sharpened the assembly sequence, with the control desk team using dozens of strain gauges, load cells, laser clearance sensors and inclination sensors sampling ten times a second — millions of data points per lift — compared in real time against finite-element predictions and prior-lift baselines to catch unexpected movement before components can clash. That accumulated experience is why the projected date for positioning the ninth and final sector module has moved up from December 2027 to mid-2027. Each sector module bundles a 440-tonne vacuum vessel sector with two ~310-tonne toroidal field coils, thermal shields and auxiliary components, all aligned to millimetre-level tolerances — the completed vacuum vessel will be 19.4 m across, 11.4 m high, and weigh roughly 5,200 tonnes before in-vessel components (8,500 tonnes after). This particular module also closes out South Korea's contribution: it is built around the fourth and final vacuum-vessel sector manufactured in Korea (Europe supplied the other five), marking the completion of Korea's vacuum vessel sector program. Compared with the previous five module installations since April 2025 (#4, #5, #6, #7, #8), this lift was executed with visibly greater speed and predictability, which ITER leadership frames as evidence the accelerated assembly approach is now working as intended on the project's schedule-critical path. For the technology, this is a concrete de-risking of ITER's construction timeline rather than a physics result: it shows that the single hardest and most schedule-sensitive phase of ITER's build — precision integration of the largest superconducting-magnet-and-vessel assemblies ever manufactured — is executing faster than planned, bringing ITER's D-D operations phase (targeted 2035) measurably closer.

**Framing.** The two primary sources (ITER's own release and World Nuclear News's photo feature) are almost entirely aligned on the facts and carry little political framing themselves — WNN's piece is purely descriptive. But the ITER release itself embeds a clear instance of the national-capability framing Asian partners typically bring: it quotes Korea's Deputy Minister of Science and ICT describing the milestone as Korea having "accumulated advanced manufacturing technologies... laying the foundation for future fusion industries," and Korea's ambassador to France calling it proof that "steady progress is being made" after a "long, difficult" road — language that reads the sector-module completion as validation of Korea's own industrial capability and its future domestic fusion programme, layered on top of the engineering milestone itself.

## 3. EAST tokamak demonstrates boronization-enabled I-mode with an expanded density window

Researchers on the EAST tokamak report the first systematic study of I-mode operation under boronized wall conditions, a confinement regime previously explored on EAST only with lithium-conditioned walls. I-mode matters for reactor design because it combines H-mode-like enhanced energy confinement with L-mode-like particle transport and is naturally ELM-free, avoiding the damaging edge-localized-mode heat pulses that plague standard H-mode operation. What changed was the wall conditioning itself: swapping lithium coating for boronization altered edge recycling behavior (evidenced by higher normalized D-alpha emission), which in turn substantially widened the density range over which I-mode could be sustained. Under boronized walls, the accessible Greenwald density fraction spans 0.26–0.77, compared with just 0.35–0.54 under the previous lithium-conditioned dataset at the same toroidal field (2.47 T) — a roughly 70% wider window at the same magnetic field strength. An even sharper contrast appeared in access to "favorable configuration" I-mode, which jumped from only 8% of lithium-conditioned discharges to 51% of boronized discharges; these favorable-configuration cases cluster at high density and show a deeper radial electric-field well with stronger E×B velocity shear, the turbulence-suppression mechanism that underlies I-mode's improved confinement. The team also derived an empirical I-mode energy confinement scaling at fixed field, showing weaker degradation with heating power and a weaker density dependence than the standard IPB98(y,2) H-mode scaling used for ITER predictions. For the technology, broadening I-mode's operating window — especially extending it to higher densities, which is where reactors need to run — makes it a more credible ELM-free scenario for future devices rather than a narrow research regime confined to a small operating island; it directly supports the case for using I-mode, not just mitigated H-mode, in reactor-relevant scenario design.

**Framing.** This result reached us only as a raw arXiv preprint from the EAST team, with no accompanying press coverage this cycle to compare against — so there's no outlet framing to contrast. Worth noting for context: EAST results are usually the ones Chinese state media frame nationally (dates, records, "China is ahead" narratives per past coverage of EAST's 403 s and 1,066 s milestones), but this particular paper is a purely technical confinement-physics report with no such framing attached.

## 4. Deuterium fusion persists at ultralow energies inside metal foils, pointing to a strong environmental screening effect

Physicists at UC Davis and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory have found that deuterium-deuterium fusion inside thin palladium and titanium foils does not fade away as expected when the incident deuteron energy is lowered — instead, the fusion rate plateaus, even at the lowest energies probed, exceeding predictions for isolated nuclei by more than 10¹⁸. Fusion reactors today operate at plasma collision energies around 10 keV; earlier experiments had shown reaction rates dropping off exponentially and effectively vanishing near 5 keV. In this experiment, using a "membrane reactor" that combines an electrochemical cell (loading deuterium into one side of a quarter-millimetre foil) with a deuterium ion beam striking the other side in vacuum, the team saw the usual exponential drop-off give way to a flat plateau below roughly 2 keV, with two independent detectors confirming the fusion products (protons and neutrons) and extra electrochemical deuterium loading roughly doubling the yield. What changed is the environment, not the fusion physics itself: unlike an isolated-nuclei plasma, a metal lattice contains electrons, structural defects and locally concentrated deuterium that combine to partially screen the Coulomb repulsion between nuclei, letting them tunnel together more easily — a "screening" effect studied since the 1990s but still not fully explained, and one that carries the field's memory of the discredited 1989 room-temperature cold-fusion claims (which the two lead researchers previously helped debunk in a Google-funded reproduction effort). Independent corroboration matters here: a European group (University of Szczecin, via the CleanHME project) reported a similar plateau in zirconium, palladium and titanium more than two years earlier using a different technique, with good agreement on the palladium results — though the two groups disagree on the mechanism, with the European group arguing the plateau reflects genuine thermal-energy fusion with an unchanged screening energy, rather than the US team's proposed enhanced screening in a damaged surface layer. For the technology, this doesn't move the needle on energy generation — the absolute fusion rate remains far too small, and both teams are explicit about that — but it opens a distinct physical regime where the surrounding material actively participates in a nuclear reaction, worth pursuing for near-term applications like compact neutron sources, and as a possible new lever (enhanced electron screening in engineered materials) for longer-term fusion research.

**Framing.** The two research groups agree on the core observation (a low-energy fusion-rate plateau, corroborated across independent experiments) but genuinely disagree on its mechanism — this is a live scientific dispute, not a manufactured one, and Physics World's coverage represents both sides fairly, including the European researcher's direct pushback on the US team's screening explanation. That even-handedness fits Physics World's usual willingness to stay skeptical rather than oversell a headline result; notably, both teams are also careful, unprompted, to distance the finding from 1989-style cold-fusion energy claims, a sign the field remains wary of repeating that credibility collapse.

## 5. DIII-D campaign comparatively qualifies 44 advanced plasma-facing materials for fusion pilot plant divertors and walls

A coordinated campaign on the DIII-D tokamak exposed and comparatively assessed 44 advanced plasma-facing materials from 12 institutions — including four public-private fusion partnerships — using the Divertor Materials Evaluation System (DiMES), explicitly to support material down-selection for near-term fusion pilot plant walls and divertors. Samples were exposed to realistic divertor-relevant conditions: Ohmic, L-mode and H-mode plasmas with edge-localized modes, at heat fluxes of 0.2–2.5 MW/m² on flush geometries and 10–15 MW/m² on 10°-angled geometries that better mimic the grazing strike-point loads a real divertor sees. What changed is the scale and coordination: rather than one lab testing one candidate material, this campaign benchmarked a wide field of tungsten-based, refractory-alloy, carbide and boron candidates side by side under identical plasma conditions, including materials contributed by private ventures alongside national-lab and ITER-grade reference samples. Results varied sharply by material family: engineered tungsten architectures broadly retained integrity, with long-fiber Wf/W showing the clearest crack-arrest behavior and W-Re and potassium-doped W performing similarly to ITER-grade tungsten, while additively-manufactured W-Ta showed heat-flux-sensitive mass loss (0.64 mg flat vs. 2.19–2.87 mg on angled geometry). Neutron-irradiated ITER-grade tungsten (0.3 dpa at 550°C) retained 2.8× more deuterium than pristine tungsten — a tritium-inventory concern for reactor operation — while TiB₂ showed the lowest deuterium release among the Ohmic-condition set. Among refractory alloys, VTaHfMo was the most stable; NbC and (Nb₀.₅Ta₀.₅)C retained integrity with only 0.02–0.03 mg mass loss versus 7 mg lost by ZrC, and CVD SiC retained macroscopic integrity but showed a silicon erosion yield roughly 5–10× above prior DIII-D trends. Renewable boron pebble rods underwent controlled recession, with about 13% of released boron ionized near the outer strike point and up to half recovered locally, and initial in-situ chromium erosion measurements were of order 10⁻². For the technology, this is a direct engineering enabler rather than a physics discovery: it gives fusion pilot-plant developers — several of them private companies racing toward first-of-a-kind devices this decade — a shared, apples-to-apples benchmark dataset for choosing plasma-facing materials, replacing scattered single-material studies with a common reference frame, and explicitly sets up future AI/ML-assisted materials screening.

**Framing.** This campaign is itself a structural example of the US framing pattern described above: it was organized around public-private partnership, with four private fusion ventures' candidate materials tested alongside national-lab and ITER reference samples in the same DIII-D shots — a commercialization-oriented, multi-company benchmarking exercise that has no direct European or Asian equivalent in this cycle's coverage. Europe's materials-qualification work tends to run centrally through EUROfusion and IPP/UKAEA facilities rather than pooling private-sector candidates in one shared national-lab campaign, and no comparable Asian materials-benchmarking release surfaced this cycle to contrast against.
