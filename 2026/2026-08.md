# Fusion Digest — August 2026

_Run: 2026-09-01_

## 1. KSTAR demonstrates real-time feedback control of ELM frequency using divertor gas puffing

Researchers on KSTAR have shown, for the first time on that device, real-time closed-loop control of edge-localized-mode (ELM) frequency using a proportional-integral (PI) feedback controller that adjusts a D₂ divertor gas puff as its actuator, run under tungsten lower-divertor conditions. ELMs are periodic edge instabilities that expel heat and particles from the plasma edge; in a tungsten-walled reactor they double as the mechanism that flushes tungsten impurities out of the plasma core, so their frequency has to be actively managed rather than left to its natural (often too-low) rate. The controller was set a genuinely dynamic target — a 30 Hz increase in ELM frequency held for 4 s, then a 30 Hz decrease held for 3 s — and tracked it with mean and median absolute percentage errors of about 13% and 12%. What changed relative to open-loop gas puffing is the closed loop itself: the controller continuously reads the measured ELM frequency and adjusts the puff rate in real time, rather than applying a fixed or pre-programmed gas schedule. The comparison that matters is against two baselines: a reference discharge with no active control, where core radiation grew exponentially as tungsten accumulated without enough ELM activity to flush it, and a discharge with excessive constant gas puffing, which suppressed tungsten buildup but degraded overall plasma performance through the extra fueling. The actively controlled shot avoided both failure modes — it held core radiation at levels comparable to the well-fueled reference case without the performance penalty of continuous heavy gas puffing. For the technology, this closes a gap between two things future tungsten-walled devices (ITER, DEMO) both need and that have historically traded off against each other: enough ELM activity to prevent core tungsten accumulation, and not so much divertor gas that confinement suffers. A working real-time controller, rather than a fixed feedforward gas recipe, is the kind of actuator-level tool that a reactor's plasma control system needs to hold that balance automatically across a discharge, and it directly targets the tungsten-erosion and core-contamination problem that is one of the harder unsolved integration issues for ITER's own tungsten divertor.

**Framing.** This result reached us as a raw arXiv preprint from the KSTAR team, with no accompanying press coverage this cycle to compare against, so there is no outlet framing to contrast directly. Worth noting for context: Korean fusion coverage typically emphasizes sustained, high-performance operation and KSTAR's contribution toward ITER's vacuum-vessel and divertor programs (Korea built four of ITER's nine vacuum-vessel sectors); this paper fits that pattern in substance even without press framing attached — it is squarely aimed at a problem ITER itself will face with its own tungsten divertor, giving Korea's programme a direct stake in the result independent of any national-milestone narrative.

**Sources.**
- "Real-time feedback control of ELM frequency using divertor gas puffing and its effects on tungsten-induced radiation and plasma performance in KSTAR" — [arXiv:2608.19259](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19259v2)

## 2. Multi-machine ELM energy-loss scaling revised, extrapolated to SPARC and ITER

A multi-machine analysis spanning seven tokamaks has revised how ELM energy loss is predicted for reactor-scale devices, replacing part of the field's long-standing reference (the 2003 Loarte scaling) with a new empirical fit tied to the separatrix turbulence parameter, a_t, rather than pedestal-top collisionality. ELM energy loss matters because each ELM dumps a burst of heat onto the divertor target; how large that burst is, normalized to the plasma's stored energy (ΔE_ELM/W_plasma), determines whether a reactor's divertor materials survive routine operation. The team assembled a database with ΔE_ELM/W_plasma ranging from 0.5% to 14% across the seven machines and found that the Loarte scaling, built around pedestal-top neoclassical collisionality, does not adequately capture the data for either peeling-ballooning-limited type-I ELMs or the smaller/quiescent-continuous-exhaust (QCE) ELM class — a real limitation for a formula that has been used for two decades to extrapolate ELM heat loads to ITER. What changed is the predictor variable: for type-I ELMs, a new regression against pedestal temperature, density, elongation and major radius yields ΔE_ELM/W_plasma of 4.5% for nominal SPARC pedestal parameters and 12% for the ITER D-T Q=10 scenario. For the small/QCE class, the team found ELM frequency rises exponentially with a_t while ΔE_ELM/W_plasma falls off exponentially, predicting much gentler losses of only 1.0% (SPARC QCE-relevant conditions) and 1.2% (ITER high-fueling scenario) — potentially below 1% if pushed further into the small/QCE regime. That is roughly a four-to-tenfold reduction in per-ELM heat load compared with the type-I estimates for the same machines. The authors are explicit that this is an initial step needing a broader dataset to firm up, but the direction of the result is unambiguous. For the technology, this reframes a design choice rather than settling it: if SPARC and ITER can be operated reliably in the small/QCE ELM regime rather than standard type-I H-mode, the divertor heat-load problem from ELMs shrinks substantially — directly informing how much active ELM mitigation (like the KSTAR-style control above) a reactor scenario actually needs, and how much margin divertor material choices have.

**Framing.** Also a single arXiv preprint this cycle, drawing on a multi-machine database rather than one national programme's device, so there is no single-country framing to apply and none to contrast against other regions. It is worth flagging as a genuinely international physics result: the machines feeding the database and the SPARC/ITER extrapolation targets span US, European and international efforts, and the paper reads as methodical scaling validation — closer to the European style of caveated, extrapolation-focused analysis than to a milestone announcement.

**Sources.**
- "First step toward multi machine ELM energy scalings and extrapolations to SPARC and ITER" — [arXiv:2608.21179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21179v1)

## 3. TCV develops a high-power scenario for alternative divertor configuration studies

The TCV tokamak (Swiss Plasma Center, EPFL) has demonstrated a new high-power operating scenario purpose-built to test alternative divertor configurations (ADCs) under boundary-plasma conditions that actually approach reactor-relevant values, something most prior ADC experiments worldwide could not do because they ran at comparatively low exhaust power. Alternative divertor geometries — snowflake, Super-X, X-divertor and similar shapes that spread or redirect the exhaust heat flux — are one of the leading physics-based candidates for solving tokamak power exhaust, but they can only be trusted as reactor solutions if tested where the heat and particle fluxes resemble what a reactor divertor will actually see. What changed is the scenario itself: 2.5 MW of electron cyclotron resonance heating fully absorbed in roughly 1 m³ of plasma, combined with high plasma current (edge safety factor q₉₅ ≈ 2.5) and a low upstream separatrix density (Greenwald fraction ≈ 0.1), together producing stationary parallel heat fluxes up to 100 MW/m² at the divertor target. That is an order of magnitude above TCV's own previous power-exhaust studies. The resulting scrape-off-layer collisionality and Lengyel detachment-scaling metric — both key parameters for whether a divertor configuration can be "detached" (radiatively cooled before it hits the target) — now fall within the range expected in SPARC, ITER and the ARC reactor concept, rather than in a lower-power regime that has to be extrapolated a long way to reach those devices. For the technology, this hands the alternative-divertor research community an actual testbed at the right operating point instead of low-power data requiring uncertain extrapolation, which should sharpen the case for — or against — specific ADC geometries as viable reactor power-exhaust solutions well before ITER or a pilot plant has to commit to one.

**Framing.** A single arXiv preprint from the TCV team this cycle, with no press coverage to compare regionally. The substance nonetheless fits the pattern European fusion research typically follows: methodical validation of a specific technical sub-problem (power exhaust) against reactor-relevant metrics, in service of design decisions for ITER and DEMO, rather than a headline record in its own right.

**Sources.**
- "High-power TCV scenario for conventional and alternative divertor studies" — [arXiv:2608.18939](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18939v1)

## 4. India commissions its first spherical tokamak at IPR

The Institute for Plasma Research (IPR) in India has completed integrated commissioning and achieved first plasma on the country's first compact spherical tokamak (ST), a low-aspect-ratio device intended to complement IPR's existing high-aspect-ratio machines, ADITYA-U and SST-1. Spherical tokamaks have a tighter, more sphere-like shape than conventional tokamaks and access physics regimes — non-inductive plasma startup, current drive in over-dense plasmas, and strongly shaped plasma boundaries — that a conventional-aspect-ratio device like ADITYA-U or SST-1 cannot study directly, so the new machine fills a genuine gap in India's experimental capability rather than duplicating it. What changed is the machine's operational status: mechanical, magnetic and electrical integration is now complete, the coil systems have passed a series of integrated commissioning tests, and first-plasma experiments have been carried out using a modest Ohmic system assisted by 2.45 GHz microwave preionization, supported by a centralized control and data-acquisition system and an initial diagnostic suite (visible imaging, spectroscopy, magnetics, radiation monitors). There is no prior IPR spherical-tokamak baseline to compare against — this is a first-of-its-kind device for the institute — so the relevant comparison is functional: it is now a working experimental platform where before there was none. For the technology, this is a capability milestone rather than a physics result: it gives India an in-house spherical-tokamak platform relevant to the broader international interest in low-aspect-ratio designs (including the UK's STEP concept and several private spherical-tokamak ventures), and opens up a genuinely new experimental campaign on non-inductive startup and shaped-plasma physics that IPR's existing devices cannot run.

**Framing.** A single arXiv preprint reporting an institute's own commissioning results, with no outside press coverage this cycle. It is a plainly technical commissioning report rather than a national-capability announcement in tone, though it sits within the broader pattern (per Appendix B) of countries building out in-house device diversity as a marker of independent fusion research capacity — worth noting without reading more national-narrative framing into the paper than the text itself supports.

**Sources.**
- "First Plasma Commissioning and Operational Highlights from India's First Spherical Tokamak at IPR" — [arXiv:2608.26024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26024v1)

## 5. SPARC's ²³⁸U fission-chamber neutron diagnostic validated against DD and DT sources

A prototype ²³⁸U-based parallel-plate fission chamber, designed for SPARC's neutron diagnostics suite, has been experimentally characterized against both deuterium-deuterium (DD) and deuterium-tritium (DT) neutron generator sources, validating a detector design SPARC will rely on to monitor its high-performance DT plasma operations. SPARC's DT campaign is expected to span an extraordinary neutron yield range, from roughly 10¹⁵ up to more than 10¹⁹ neutrons per second, so the diagnostic covering that range has to be proven reliable across many orders of magnitude before routine DT operation can begin. What changed is that this design — a parallel-plate detector geometry paired with borated polyethylene collimation intended to prioritize unscattered DD and DT fusion neutrons over scattered background — has now actually been tested rather than only simulated: experimental runs against both DD and DT generators confirmed vendor-specified detector efficiencies, demonstrated good count-rate linearity, and matched OpenMC neutronics simulations. The team also stress-tested the design against SPARC-specific environmental conditions no laboratory bench test would otherwise catch — stray magnetic fields up to 14 mT and signal degradation risk over roughly 30 m of cable run between detector and electronics — and found the chamber robust to both, when paired with appropriate shielding and pulse-height thresholds. There is no earlier SPARC-specific fission-chamber baseline to compare against, since this is the first experimental characterization of the concept for that device; the comparison here is to the design's own specifications and to independent OpenMC simulation, both of which the hardware met. For the technology, reliable real-time neutron and fusion-power monitoring is a prerequisite — for machine protection, physics analysis, and licensing — of any high-performance DT campaign, so this closes a specific instrumentation gap on the path to SPARC's planned DT operation later this decade, ahead of the physics results that diagnostic will eventually be used to measure.

**Framing.** A single arXiv preprint from the SPARC diagnostics team, with no press coverage this cycle to compare. Structurally it fits the US pattern of commercialization-oriented, deadline-driven engineering work — building and validating a specific piece of hardware SPARC needs on a fixed schedule — more than the European style of open-ended physics validation, though the paper itself is presented in plainly technical terms with no commercial framing attached.

**Sources.**
- "Characterization of a prototype parallel-plate ²³⁸U fission chamber with DD and DT fusion neutron sources" — [arXiv:2608.07466](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07466v1)
